## IPA Is a Tool, Not a New Language

English spelling does not always show pronunciation clearly. The same letters can represent different sounds:

> `cat` /kæt/  
> `call` /kɔl/  
> `about` /əˈbaʊt/

IPA, the International Phonetic Alphabet, gives one symbol or symbol group for a sound. Slashes show pronunciation: `/kæt/`. The mark `/ˈ/` appears before the stressed syllable: `/əˈbaʊt/` means a**BOUT**.

You do not need to memorize the whole IPA chart now. Use it to answer a question:

- Are these two words pronounced differently?
- Which vowel do I need?
- Which syllable is stressed?
- Does the dictionary show an American or British variant?

This course uses contemporary General American pronunciation as its main reference. Other standard accents are normal and useful to understand.

## Notice Sounds That Change Meaning

A minimal pair contains two words that differ by one sound. Hearing the contrast is more important than describing every movement of the mouth.

#### Four Useful Contrasts

| Contrast | Word 1 | Word 2 | Main clue |
|---|---|---|---|
| /ɪ/ and /i/ | `ship` /ʃɪp/ | `sheep` /ʃip/ | the vowel in **sheep** is tenser and usually longer |
| /æ/ and /ɛ/ | `bad` /bæd/ | `bed` /bɛd/ | **bad** has a more open vowel |
| /ʌ/ and /æ/ | `cut` /kʌt/ | `cat` /kæt/ | do not rely on the letter alone |
| /w/ and /v/ | `west` /wɛst/ | `vest` /vɛst/ | /w/ has rounded lips; /v/ uses the lower lip and upper teeth |

Vowel length varies with context and accent. Treat “longer” as a listening clue here, not as a mechanical rule.

## Find the Stressed Syllable

In a word with two or more syllables, one syllable is usually more prominent. It is often clearer, stronger and longer than the others. Dictionary entries mark primary stress with `/ˈ/` before that syllable.

#### Stress Models

> `TAble` /ˈteɪbəl/  
> `hoTEL` /hoʊˈtɛl/  
> `PHOtograph` /ˈfoʊtəˌɡræf/  
> `phoTOGraphy` /fəˈtɑɡrəfi/  
> `REcord` noun /ˈrekərd/  
> `reCORD` verb /rɪˈkɔrd/

Capital letters above are a temporary visual aid, not normal spelling. In IPA, `/ˌ/` marks secondary stress, which is weaker than primary stress.

Unstressed vowels are often reduced. The most common reduced vowel is schwa `/ə/`, heard at the start of `about` /əˈbaʊt/ and in the final syllable of `teacher` /ˈtitʃər/ in General American English.

## Stress the Important Words in a Sentence

English does not normally give every word equal prominence. Content words that carry the main message are often stressed; grammar words are often weaker. The speaker can move stress to show a contrast.

#### Neutral and Contrastive Stress

> I **NEED** a new **CHARger**.  
> I need a **NEW** charger. — not the old one  
> **I** need a new charger. — not another person

In neutral speech, `I`, `a` and many other small grammar words are usually less prominent. Do not delete them. Say them more lightly so the important words stand out.

### Task 7. Choose the Main Stress

Which word should receive strong contrastive stress?

1. You need a blue charger, not a black one: `I need a blue charger.`
2. You need the charger today, not tomorrow: `I need the charger today.`
3. You, not your brother, need it: `I need a charger.`
4. You need to borrow it, not buy it: `I need to borrow a charger.`

Read each sentence twice: first neutrally, then with the contrastive stress.

## Prepare Your First Recording

You need any voice recorder that lets you replay your speech. The recording is private unless you choose to share it.

#### Recording Text

> Hi, I'm Alex. I'm learning English because I want to understand more and communicate clearly. I usually study at home, and I make time for a short lesson in the evening. Pronunciation is sometimes difficult, but I pay attention to stress and useful examples. Today I'm recording my voice for the first time. I don't need to sound perfect. I want my words and main ideas to be clear.

The name and personal details may be changed. Before recording:

1. Underline the words that carry the main information.
2. Mark the stressed syllable in any word you are unsure about.
3. Check no more than three words in a learner's dictionary.
4. Read the text silently once, then aloud once.

### Task 8. Record Version One

Record the text without stopping after every small mistake. Then replay it once and answer:

1. Can you understand every sentence without reading?
2. Which two or three words are least clear?
3. Does one important word stand out in each sentence?
4. Are pauses mostly between ideas rather than inside short phrases?

Do not restart merely because your accent is not native-like.

## Improve One Thing at a Time

Choose no more than three changes. Useful changes include:

- correcting the stressed syllable in one word;
- making a key consonant or vowel clearer;
- stressing an important word in a sentence;
- making a grammar word lighter;
- pausing at the end of an idea;
- reading a short phrase as one unit instead of word by word.

### Task 9. Record Version Two

Practice only the selected words or phrases, then record the complete text again. Compare the two versions.

1. Name one specific improvement you can hear.
2. Name one feature to revisit later.
3. Which version communicates the message more clearly?

Keep both recordings if convenient. They are a starting point, not a test score.
