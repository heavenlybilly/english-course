## Read for Meaning Before Vocabulary

Read the text once without a dictionary. On the first reading, aim to understand the situation, change and result.

#### Short Text: The Saturday Workshop

> On Saturday morning, Nina carried an old wooden chair into a community workshop. One leg was loose, the seat was scratched, and the chair made an unpleasant sound whenever anyone moved it. Nina had found it beside the bins behind her apartment building. She liked its shape, but she had never repaired furniture before.
>
> The workshop was already busy. At one table, two people were fixing lamps. Near the window, a woman was showing a teenager how to sew a torn backpack. Nobody looked like a professional, but everyone was sharing tools and advice.
>
> A volunteer named Sam examined Nina's chair. He did not repair it for her. Instead, he asked her to move each leg and find the source of the problem. One wooden joint had become loose. Sam showed her how to remove the old glue, apply a small amount of new glue and hold the joint in place with a clamp.
>
> Nina wanted to finish quickly, but the glue needed time to dry. While she waited, she helped another visitor replace a broken drawer handle. At first, she worried that she would make the damage worse. The visitor explained each step, and together they finished the repair.
>
> By the end of the afternoon, Nina's chair was stable again. The scratches were still visible, but she decided to keep them. They were part of the chair's history. Nina arrived hoping that someone would fix one object for her. She left knowing that she could learn to repair things herself—and that asking for guidance was different from asking someone else to do all the work.

### Task 2. Choose the Main Idea

Choose the best summary.

A. Nina learns that old furniture should always look new after a repair.

B. Nina repairs a chair and begins to see herself as someone who can learn practical skills.

C. Nina decides that only professionals should use workshop tools.

### Task 3. Find Evidence and Detail

Answer in English.

1. Where did Nina find the chair?
2. Name two problems with it.
3. What were people repairing near the window?
4. Why did Sam ask Nina to move each leg?
5. What did she use to hold the repaired joint in place?
6. Why could she not finish the chair immediately?
7. What other repair did she help with?
8. What important idea did Nina learn by the end?

### Task 4. Make Vocabulary Decisions

For each item, choose **ignore**, **guess**, **check later** or **look up now** for the stated purpose. Explain briefly.

1. You only need the main idea. You do not know **scratched**, but you understand that the chair is old and damaged.
2. You must explain exactly how the joint was held while the glue dried. You do not know **clamp**.
3. You guess that **torn** describes damage to the backpack and want to use it in your own sentence.
4. You do not know the exact meaning of **bins**, but you know where Nina found the discarded chair and the detail is not important to your response.

## Use Dictionary Evidence

#### Simplified Entries

> **repair** /rɪˈpɛr/ **verb [T]** — to fix something that is damaged: *They repaired the roof.*
> **repair** /rɪˈpɛr/ **noun [C/U]** — the act or result of fixing something: *The chair needs a simple repair.*
>
> **damage** /ˈdæmɪdʒ/ **noun [U]** — physical harm: *The storm caused serious damage.*
> **damage** /ˈdæmɪdʒ/ **verb [T]** — to harm something physically: *Water damaged the floor.*

### Task 6. Read the Entries Carefully

1. Is **repair** a noun or verb in `They repaired the chair`?
2. Is **repair** countable in `The chair needs a repair`?
3. Correct: `The storm caused several damages.`
4. Complete: Water `_____` the wooden seat yesterday. (`damage`)
5. Where is the stress in **repair**?
6. Where is the stress in **damage**?
7. Write one original sentence with either word and identify its part of speech.

## Write Clearly Without Stopping for Every Gap

### Task 11. Write a Request for Guidance

Write a friendly message of 80–120 words. Choose a practical or creative skill you want to learn.

Include:

- the skill;
- why it matters to you;
- one concrete difficulty;
- what you have already tried;
- one specific request for guidance;
- a simple description if you cannot recall an exact word.

Write the first version without a dictionary. Revise it yourself, then check no more than three words that still affect clarity.

**Self-check**

- The reader can understand my goal and difficulty.
- The request can receive a specific answer.
- Every sentence has a clear beginning and end.
- Capitals and punctuation make the message easy to read.
- I used a description instead of inventing an English word.

## Make a Short Recording

Speaking remains optional at this stage, but the recording gives useful evidence about clarity.

### Task 12. Explain What You Want to Learn

Using keywords rather than a full script, record 45–60 seconds about the same skill as Task 11. Include the goal, difficulty, previous attempt and request.

Replay it once. Choose no more than three changes involving unclear key words, stress, pauses or missing information. Record a second version.

Then answer:

1. What became clearer?
2. What should you revisit later?
3. Which version communicates the main idea better?
